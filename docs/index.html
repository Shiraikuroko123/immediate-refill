When is immediate refill optimal for parallel voting?

Launching one more reasoning path can slow every path already running. We characterize when filling a free slot is fastest, and test the prediction on an NVIDIA A800.

The optimality condition

The shape of the decoding cost curve

In parallel voting, a model answers the same question along several paths. Each finished path casts a vote. Once the leader’s margin exceeds the number of outstanding votes, the remaining paths cannot change the winner. Until then, whenever a path finishes, the scheduler must decide what to do with the free slot.

Suppose requests start in a fixed order and no extra launches are allowed beyond the serial voting budget. For a fixed panel size, slot count and launch quota, immediate refill is fastest on every input exactly when the step costs are discretely concave over the reachable widths.

What if the curve is not concave?

Keep the slot count and the step costs fixed, and allow panel size and launch quota to vary. The largest ratio of the least concave majorant to the actual cost gives the exact worst-case latency ratio for immediate refill.

When can the next path start?

A free slot gives the scheduler two decisions. Starting another path must respect the caller’s budget. Once the budget permits it, the scheduler can choose when to start.

Two votes unlock the fifth path

One question has five candidate paths (N = 5), numbered in launch order. At most three can run at once (P = 3). The caller allows at most five launches (Q = 5), and only one more than serial voting would need (B = 1). If serial voting would stop after K paths, even paths later canceled count toward that K + 1 limit. We can constrain launches before knowing K.

  1. Paths 1 and 4 are running. Path 2 voted A and path 3 voted B. Four paths have started and one slot is free.
  2. Whatever path 1 returns, the leader after the first three votes can lead by at most one. Two votes remain, so those first three cannot certify a winner. The earliest possible serial stop is now four. For example, votes A, A, B, A give A a two-vote lead with only one vote left.
  3. The budget therefore permits 4 + 1 = 5 starts. A scheduler that uses the returned positions can launch path 5. One that buffers votes until path 1 returns still has an empty contiguous prefix. From that prefix alone, three unanimous votes remain possible. Its safe limit is 3 + 1 = 4.

In general, let M be the earliest serial stopping position consistent with the votes seen so far. A launch must satisfy all three limits at once. Total starts cannot exceed Q or M + B, and active paths cannot exceed P. Immediate refill starts as many paths as those limits allow. English paper, §2.2, pp. 2–3

Why the cost of one more active path matters

In one virtual decoding step, each active path advances by one unit. With k active paths that step takes ck units of real time. With zero extra launches (B = 0), every valid schedule must finish the same prefix. Each has the same work to do. Their difference is how that work is distributed across widths.

Take the busiest intervals of a competing schedule, even if they are separated by gaps. Immediate refill does at least as much work in its first interval of the same total virtual duration. The proof compresses the gaps while preserving the information available at each admission. It uses a special feature of the vote budget. Each completion frees at most one further launch, so the permitted width can only shrink.

With N, Q and P fixed, immediate refill is fastest for every input exactly when each extra active path adds no more step cost than the previous one, across reachable widths. This is discrete concavity, with c0 = 0. When only slot count and width costs are fixed and panel size and quota may vary, the tight worst-case ratio is the largest ratio of the least concave majorant to the original cost. The majorant is the lowest concave curve above all cost points.

Chang and Yao study rearrangements of given vectors. Here the votes determine which paths can start. Once we compare the resulting occupancies, classical majorization gives the ordering for concave costs. What has to be proved here is the occupancy comparison itself when returned votes control admission, and that voting panels can approach the claimed worst case. A four-task example in Appendix J shows why earlier completion of each task alone is insufficient. Theorem 1, pp. 3–4 · Appendix J, p. 16

A lower bound once extra launches are allowed

With a positive extra budget, different sets of returned votes can certify the winner. Suppose a schedule needs path 4, which has length 100. Paths 1–3 started no later. By the time path 4 finishes, each earlier path has received at least 100 units of work or has already finished. Charging only the paths that returned would miss some of that work.

For each possible set of completions at return, a linear program prices the minimum width durations needed to deliver this work and meet the earliest completion times. Taking the minimum across a family that covers every realizable set gives a lower bound L on elapsed time. If immediate refill takes T, even a scheduler that knows future lengths and votes can save at most 1 − L/T while obeying the same admission rule. A bound may allow width profiles that no schedule can realize. It applies to the specified workloads, votes and shared clock. Theorem 2, p. 5

Limits of the measurements

In a separate fixed-workload A800 comparison of admission policies on 16 problems, the mean saving was 0.55% (95% interval −0.03% to 1.29%). For 200 archived three-request panels, a length-aware choice between immediate launch and the first completion saved 0.364% on average. Each comparison uses its own baseline.

Experiments

From a decoding step to a measured latency

Can a short calibration predict when to wait?

On one A800 running Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with 512-token prompts, each of eight calibration blocks tested widths 1, 2 and 3 once in shuffled order. After 64 untimed decode steps, each window timed 96 cached steps. For each width, the median of the eight window means supplied the step cost. An initial warmup block was excluded. The first process sealed both predictions before a fresh process ran 12 validation blocks. Each block tested both launch policies on both workloads, timing from first submission through GPU synchronization after the last completion.

For lengths 256, 128 and 128, delaying request three reduced median latency by 2.46% against the predicted 3.13%. With two equal 128-token requests, delaying the second forced serial execution. Median latency rose by 102.63% against a predicted 104.30%.

How did a prediction qualify as correct?

The protocol ranked the 12 paired latency savings and used the interval from the third to the tenth value. When the magnitude of the predicted saving was at least 1%, that interval had to lie strictly on the predicted side of zero. For a smaller prediction, the entire interval had to lie within −2% to +2%. In both cases, the median had to be within two percentage points of the prediction. The three-request experiment and the two-request control both made directional predictions and both passed.

Do the gains carry over to generated answers?

On 16 hash-selected GSM8K problems, fixed-workload A800 runs changed the launch schedule on four problems and saved 0.55% on average (95% interval −0.03% to 1.29%). The protocol had set a 2% practical threshold. With natural sampling the mean was 0.48% (−0.04% to 1.30%). Each condition used one timed pair per problem within one session.

A second study paired two output caps on 32 problems. Raising the cap from 384 to 1,024 tokens changed mean measured savings from 0.86% to 1.30%. The paired difference was +0.44 percentage points (95% interval −0.79 to +1.84), leaving its direction unresolved. For a serving system, a direct check is to record whether out-of-prefix votes change any launch, then time those policies end to end under the intended traffic. Queueing, concurrent users and timing variation across sessions remain open measurements.
